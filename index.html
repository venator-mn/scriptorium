What This Is

A Room for Working the Text

✦ ✦ ✦

A scriptorium was the room in a monastery where texts were copied, studied, and annotated by hand, a working space for close attention to Scripture, not a display case for it. That's what this page is: a room with three tools in it, not three separate projects.

Inductio is the method, the discipline of observation, interpretation, and application that structures the actual work of study. SOAQ is what you use while you're doing it, a journaling framework for getting what you're seeing onto the page. Lectio Continua is the reading plan that decides where to point all of it each day.

A Method for Personal Bible Study

Inductio

Observe what it says, before deciding what it means

Observation · Interpretation · Application

Overview

Three Steps, Not Three Stages

✦ ✦ ✦

The method has three steps: observation, interpretation, application. They're numbered here for clarity, not because you move through them once in strict order. In practice you circle back constantly, an observation two verses later reshapes an interpretation you already formed, and an application question sends you back to reread the text more carefully.

None of it substitutes for prayer. No method sharpens your eyes on its own, this is a set of habits for paying closer attention to the text, not a mechanism that produces understanding by itself.

Step One

Observation — What Does the Text Say?

✦ ✦ ✦

Before you interpret anything, slow down and register what's actually on the page. Ask the standard questions of every passage, who, what, when, where, why, how, and let the following categories train your eye to notice what's easy to read past.

Key words

Mark key words and synonyms

A word the author leans on repeatedly is usually carrying his point. Track where it appears and how its use shifts across the passage.

Lists

Make lists

When a passage enumerates things, pull the list out and number it. Also list what you learn about any key word or person as you go.

Contrasts

Watch for contrasts and comparisons

Where the author sets two things against each other, or reaches for an image to describe something, that's usually load-bearing for the meaning.

Conclusions

Mark terms of conclusion

"Therefore," "thus," "for this reason," these signal the author summing up an argument. Often the clearest single statement of his point.

Time

Note expressions of time

Sequence and timing carry real interpretive weight, especially in narrative and prophecy. Mark "when," "after," "then," and the like.

Geography

Mark geographic locations

Where an event happens can matter for understanding it, and it's easy to skim past.

Speaker

Mark author and recipients separately

In epistles especially, keep track of who's writing and who's being addressed. It clarifies pronouns and grounds the letter's occasion.

Identify the theme

Once you've done the above, state the theme of the chapter or section as briefly as you can, ideally in the text's own words.

Step Two

Interpretation — What Does It Mean?

✦ ✦ ✦

Observation tells you what's on the page. Interpretation is the harder work of understanding what it means, and it's where careless reading does the most damage. Six guidelines:

01

Context rules

Read every verse against the verses around it, the book it's in, and the whole of Scripture. Don't pull a line out of its setting to make it say what you want.

02

Seek the full counsel of Scripture

Don't build a conviction on one or two isolated verses. Knowing the text broadly is what lets you recognize when a teaching doesn't hold up.

03

Scripture doesn't contradict itself

Where two truths seem in tension, don't resolve it by pushing either one further than the text itself does. Sometimes you hold both without full resolution.

04

Don't build on obscure passages

If a passage stays genuinely unclear after careful reading, it's not a solid foundation for a position, however appealing.

05

Take the plain sense, and mind the genre

Look for what's clearly said before hunting for a hidden meaning. Poetry and prophecy carry more figurative language than narrative or teaching, read accordingly.

06

Look for the single meaning intended

Unless the author himself signals a second layer, don't multiply interpretations. The goal is recovering what he meant.

Interpretation is where a method can't do the work for you. It narrows the range of responsible readings, it doesn't hand you the answer.

Step Three

Application — What Now?

✦ ✦ ✦

Application only works once you actually know what the text says and what it means. At that point the question shifts from understanding to obedience, whether you'll live differently in light of it.

The logic behind this, echoing 2 Timothy 3:16–17: Scripture teaches what's right, exposes where you're wrong, shows the way to correct it, and trains you toward right living, so you're equipped for whatever's in front of you.

Illustration

Worked Example — John 6:29–35

✦ ✦ ✦

A short pass through the method on one passage.

John 6:29–35 KJV
Observe
The crowd asks for a sign, invoking the manna their fathers ate in the wilderness. Jesus corrects the attribution: it wasn't Moses who gave that bread, but the Father, and identifies "the true bread from heaven" as the one who comes down and gives life to the world. They ask for it. He answers, "I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst."
Interpret
The contrast carries the meaning: the crowd wants more of what they already had, bread on tap, and Jesus redirects them to something categorically different. "Coming" and "believing" are placed in parallel in verse 35, suggesting they're being used as near-synonyms here, not two separate conditions.
Apply
The pull to want some tangible sign or "thing" to make belief easier isn't unique to that crowd. The passage pushes back on that impulse directly, Jesus presents himself as sufficient, not as one input among others toward believing.

A Bible Journaling Framework

SOAQ

Soak in the Word — chapter by chapter

Scripture · Observation · Application · Questions

Overview

What is SOAQ?

✦ ✦ ✦

SOAQ is a simple, repeatable framework for journaling through the Bible chapter by chapter. The name is intentional — the goal is to soak in the text, not rush through it. Each letter represents a distinct mode of engagement with what you have read.

SOAQ is designed for physical journaling. There is no form to fill out. The four letters become internalized after a handful of entries, and from that point you simply write — using the letters as mental anchors rather than rigid sections.

"The four letters become internalized quickly. Within a week of consistent use, you will reach for them without thinking — the way a musician reaches for a key signature."

Before You Begin

Starting Each Entry

✦ ✦ ✦

Before you write anything, jot a simple three-part header at the top of the page. This takes ten seconds and gives every entry a consistent identity you can scan later.

Book & Chapter

e.g. Gen 3

Date

e.g. Apr 2, 2026

One-phrase title

e.g. The Fall

The one-phrase title is your own summary of the chapter in plain words. It forces you to identify what the chapter is actually about before you begin writing — a discipline in itself.

The Four Elements

✦ ✦ ✦
S

Scripture

Write out the key verse or short passage that anchors the chapter for you.

  • Choose one to three verses that best capture the heart of the chapter
  • Write them out by hand — the act of transcription is itself formative
  • Include the reference (book, chapter, verse) and your translation
  • For narrative chapters, a brief quote or paraphrase is fine
O

Observation

What does the text actually say? Plain reading only — no interpretation yet.

  • Who are the people involved? What happens, and in what order?
  • What is the setting, tone, or structure of the passage?
  • What words, phrases, or details stand out as significant?
  • For genealogy or law chapters: list key names, lineages, or commands
A

Application

What does this mean, and what do you do with it? Interpretation and personal response together.

  • What is the central message or theme of this chapter?
  • What does this reveal about God's character, his promises, or his purposes?
  • Where does this text meet your life right now — conviction, encouragement, challenge?
  • Is there a sin to confess, a promise to trust, or a command to obey?
Q

Questions

Capture what you do not yet understand. These are the seeds of deeper study.

  • What confused, surprised, or unsettled you in this chapter?
  • What word, name, or concept deserves further research?
  • What tension does this create with other passages you know?
  • Note the reference so you can return to it with a concordance or commentary

Guidance

How to Use It in Practice

✦ ✦ ✦

Write freely — tag as you go

You do not need to write in four separate blocks. Many entries will flow naturally as continuous prose. Jot the letter S, O, A, or Q in the margin next to the relevant paragraph as you write.

Observation before interpretation

Resist the urge to interpret while still recording. Let the text speak on its own terms first. Your Application section deepens considerably when built on careful Observation.

Questions are not failures

A rich Q section is a sign of serious reading. Honest questions kept over time become a personal theology journal — a record of where God has taught you and where you are still waiting for light.

Prayer is assumed

SOAQ does not include a formal prayer section because prayer is not a step — it is the posture of the whole exercise. Let it arise naturally from what you have written.

Catalog notes in the back

Genealogies, place names, census figures, and legal codes belong in a dedicated section at the back of your journal — not inside SOAQ entries, where they interrupt the flow of reflection.

One phrase title matters

Writing a chapter title before you begin forces distillation. Over months of entries you will have a personally-authored outline of the entire Bible in your own words.

Illustration

Sample Entry — Genesis 3

✦ ✦ ✦

The following illustrates what a completed SOAQ entry might look like in practice.

Gen 3 | Apr 2, 2026 | The Fall
S
"You shall not eat of any tree in the garden…you will not surely die. For God knows that when you eat of it your eyes will be opened, and you will be like God." — Gen 3:1–5 (LSB)
O
The serpent questions God's command, then contradicts it directly. Eve engages the dialogue rather than ending it. The fruit is described three ways — good for food, a delight to the eyes, desirable for wisdom — covering physical, aesthetic, and intellectual appeal. Adam is present but silent. God walks in the garden in the cool of the day; the hiding is the first evidence of shame. Curses are pronounced in reverse order: serpent, woman, man, ground.
A
Temptation rarely looks like temptation. The serpent doesn't deny God's existence — it reframes what God said and questions his motives. I am vulnerable to the same move: rationalizing what I want by subtly distorting what I know to be true. The triple appeal of the fruit (body, eye, pride) maps closely to what John calls the world in 1 John 2:16. The garments God makes in v.21 are the first sacrifice — an unexpected act of grace inside a passage of judgment.
Q
Why does God allow the serpent in the garden at all? What does it mean that their eyes "were opened" — was there something genuinely new they perceived, or only shame? Look up Ezek 28 and compare with the Eden narrative. How does the protevangelium (v.15) relate to the seed language in Galatians?

Build Your Plan

Choose Your Pace

✦ ✦ ✦

Pick a canon and how many times you'd like to read through each track (History, Psalms, Wisdom, Prophets, and New Testament) this year. History runs from Genesis through Esther; Wisdom covers Job, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Song of Solomon (plus Wisdom and Sirach in the Catholic canon); Prophets covers Isaiah through Malachi. This plan is built around finishing chapters, books and tracks, not spreading evenly verse by verse.

In practice, every day rounds up to a full chapter rather than stopping mid-thought. By default all five tracks are synchronized to finish together: day 1 and the final day always carry a reading from every track, and shorter tracks pick up occasional off days so they stretch to match the longest one. Uncheck the synchronize option below if you'd rather have each track run at its own pace and go quiet with a simple em dash once it's done, that's not a flaw to smooth over either, it's what gives you the freedom to decide, track by track, how much of this year belongs to each part of the story.
